PROLOGUE — A SUMMER THAT NEVER ENDED

Somewhere in a 1994 that never quite finished, the sky is always blue and the grass is always freshly mown in bright stripes. Shirts are baggy, collars are big, hair is bigger, and every match is shown on a television with a small score box in the corner.

In this summer, a championship is announced in a Japan of the imagination. Its stadiums have names like the Big Ramen Bowl and the Mochi Mochi Dome. Its cities are called Tokyo-yo, Yokohamham and Osaka-ka. Forty-eight nations want to be there: Sambalia and Argentango, Baguettia and Pastalia, Paellania and Wurstland, Sushipon and Kimchirea, Jollofia and Senegoal, Burgerland and Mapledonia, and many more, each proud of the dish, the dance or the landscape it was named after.

Only thirty-two can play in the finals. The championship is called the Road to 2030, because every great football journey is longer than ninety minutes.

Eleven men pull on your nation's shirt. They are not famous yet. That is about to change.

CHAPTER ONE — THE QUALIFYING CAMPAIGN

Before a single ball is kicked in Japan, the forty-eight nations go through a long qualifying campaign. Strength matters. Luck matters too. The great football nations nearly always come through, but every few summers a giant stays at home and a small nation packs its boots with disbelief.

Your nation does not have to worry about that part: it has been invited as a guest of honour. The others are not so lucky. When the list of thirty-two is read out, there are celebrations in some capitals and long silences in others.

In the dressing room, your coach has already started shouting. He has not yet decided what about.

CHAPTER TWO — THE DRAW

The draw takes place under bright studio lights. Thirty-two names go into the pots, and eight groups of four come out: Group A to Group H.

Your eleven watch on a small television with the sound turned up too loud. Every name is greeted with a groan, a cheer or a nervous laugh. A group with Sambalia in it is called "the group of death" by everybody, including Sambalia.

Then the schedule appears. Three group matches. Three chances. The top two in each group go on to the knock-out rounds; the others go home with a souvenir and a story.

CHAPTER THREE — THE OFFICIALS

Three referees will share the championship, and everyone has an opinion about them.

Mr Kaminari loves cards. Yellow ones, red ones, and probably cards he has invented himself. He writes names in his notebook slowly, and he asks players to spell them.

Mr Sato is fair and short. Nobody can argue with his decisions, partly because nobody can see him in a crowd of defenders.

Mr Tanaka naps a lot. He is a very good referee whenever he is awake.

The linesmen apologise constantly and wear glasses. The physio carries a magic sponge that cures everything except a bad pass. And for the modern rules there is a video room, where a tiny referee squints at a very big monitor while the whole stadium waits.

Each side may ask for a video review twice in a match. The review never guesses: it looks at what really happened. A good goal stands. An offside goal does not. The call is made, and the crowd either explodes or groans.

CHAPTER FOUR — THE GROUP STAGE

The first match is in a stadium full of colour. Both nations' supporters wave flags in their own shirts' colours, and they sing their own songs. When your eleven attack, the noise rises. When they are in danger, it swells into a roar. When the referee gives a strange free kick, the whistling begins.

Football is simple, and football is difficult. A short pass must reach a man, not the space beside him. A long ball must drop where he arrives. A shot can be placed with a tap or struck with everything a striker has. The keeper must be beaten: he dives, he punches crosses away, he catches what he can, and he complains when he cannot.

When the first goal comes, the whole screen agrees:

GOAL! GOAL! GOAL!

The scorer runs to the supporters to celebrate in his nation's own style: a samba, a knee slide, the shirt pulled over his head, a baby rocked in his arms, a belly slide across the wet grass, a prayer, a scream at the sky or a perfect backflip. His team-mates pile on. The losing side hangs its head. Then the replay shows the goal again, just in case anybody missed it.

The coach appears in his little television window. Sometimes he is delighted: "I taught him that!" Sometimes he is not: "My grandma scores that!" Once, after a miss from a metre, he simply says: "Why?! Why?! Why?!"

Three matches later, the group table tells the truth: played, won, drawn, lost, goal difference and points. Points come first, then goal difference, then goals scored. There is no arguing with the table, although several coaches try.

CHAPTER FIVE — BOOKINGS AND THE BENCH

Championships are not only won by goals. A clumsy slide from behind earns a yellow card. Two in the championship, or a single red, and that man sits out the next match.

The suspended player watches from the stand in his tracksuit. The bench sends someone else. The substitute warms up on the touchline and says what every substitute says: "Finally! My legs were cold!"

The line-up screen before each match shows who can play and who cannot. Every choice in the group stage follows your eleven into the knock-out rounds.

CHAPTER SIX — THE KNOCK-OUT

From the last sixteen on, there is no table to save anyone. Group winners face the runners-up of another group. A draw is no longer an answer: if the match is level, it goes to extra time, perhaps to a golden goal, and finally to penalties.

The penalty is the purest moment in football. The camera drops low behind the spot. The taker sees only the keeper, the goal and his own shaking boots. The keeper shuffles along his line, guesses, and dives. Go too early, and the taker may see it. Wait too long, and the ball is already in the net.

The bracket fills in after every round: the last sixteen, the quarter-finals and the semi-finals. Thirty-two nations become sixteen, then eight, then four.

Somewhere in the semi-final, the striker who has scored the most goals starts to hear his name chanted in a language he does not speak. The golden boot is in sight.

CHAPTER SEVEN — THE FINAL AT THE BIG RAMEN BOWL

The semi-finals' losers play for third place, and they play for pride, which is sometimes more dangerous than playing for a trophy.

The final is in Tokyo-yo, at the Big Ramen Bowl, the largest stadium of the championship. Before kick-off the music changes. It is bigger now: brass, drums and a melody that sounds like a summer that will never come back.

Your eleven walk out under a sky that is still blue. The other finalist is the best side left in the world. The referee checks his whistle twice. The coach checks his tie three times. Every supporter in the stadium is standing.

What happens next is up to the player.

ENDING — CHAMPIONS OF THE ROAD TO 2030

If your eleven win the final, the screen says what everybody already knows:

CHAMPIONS OF THE ROAD TO 2030

The trophy ceremony begins. Five of the champions dance around the cup. One cries. One sings. One tries to take the trophy home in his kit bag. The golden boot is announced, and the championship's top scorer pretends to be surprised.

In the dressing room, the coach is finally quiet. He wipes his glasses, looks at his players and says the only thing he has never said before: "Well done."

ENDING — THE ROAD ENDS HERE

If your eleven are beaten, the road ends there. There is no shame in it. Somebody must lose every match, and the summer will come round again.

The supporters fold their flags slowly. The players shake hands with the winners. The coach is already planning the next campaign, and already shouting about it.

EPILOGUE — ONE MORE SUMMER

The stadiums empty. The grass is mown again into bright stripes. In the morning, a new championship will be drawn, with new groups, new rivals and perhaps a giant left at home.

Eleven new men are already lacing their boots.

The road to 2030 never really ends. It only goes round once more.
